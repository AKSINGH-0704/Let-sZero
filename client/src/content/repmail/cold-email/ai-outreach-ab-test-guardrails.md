Set firm guardrails before running A/B tests that use AI to personalize outreach. Define which sensitive traits are off-limits, document evidence requirements for any factual claims the model will produce, and require human review checkpoints for flagged outputs. These steps prevent harmful experimentation and keep tests defensible to auditors and internal stakeholders.

## Decision boundary: what experiments are allowed vs. blocked

Define allowed experiments by explicit data and feature types (e.g., firmographic data, publicly-stated role, recent product activity). Block experiments that infer or target protected classes (race, ethnicity, religion, sexual orientation), health status, or criminal history unless you have express, documented consent and legal sign-off. Explicitly treat model-generated assertions about people’s identity or sensitive circumstances as disallowed outputs.

Document boundary rationale: privacy risk, regulatory scrutiny, and brand harm. Cite relevant guidance when asserting regulatory relevance (directional references only) — e.g., advertising/marketing responsibilities in the U.S. may apply to claims made about people or products [1]. When in doubt, classify the experiment as blocked until legal or privacy review clears it.

## Evidence limits: what the model can claim and how to support it

For any factual claim the model will generate about a recipient (employment, company size, actions), require a one-source minimum that meets defined quality criteria (timestamp, source type such as company site, verified API, or public filing). Do not accept model hallucinations or single social-media inferences for high-stakes claims.

Where claims derive from model reasoning rather than direct data, require language templates that either (a) label the content as an inference and state uncertainty, or (b) avoid the claim entirely. Link claims to the underlying evidence in test documentation so reviewers can reproduce the source check quickly.

## Practical sequence: how to run a compliant A/B test

1) Pre-test: register the experiment in a central tracker with hypothesis, features used, data sources, and owner. 2) Policy review: route tests that use new data types or that touch sensitive categories to privacy and legal for sign-off. 3) Synthetic dry-run: generate a limited sample of outputs and run a human review on each variant to mark safety issues before wide deployment.

Only after passing these steps should you deploy the test to a controlled sample (e.g., <1% of sends or a capped recipient count). Monitor flagged metrics in real time (recipient complaints, unsubscribes, or QA flags) and stop immediately if thresholds are crossed.

## Human review and escalation: roles, checklists, and stop conditions

Assign a named reviewer who inspects AI-generated language for unsupported claims, sensitive-tailoring, or privacy exposures. Use a short checklist during review: claim supported by citation, no inference of protected attributes, and phrasing shows uncertainty when warranted. The reviewer must record a pass/fail decision and timestamp in the experiment log.

Define stop conditions in advance: any single outbound that contains an unsupported claim about a recipient or any pattern of >X complaints per Y sends (choose X/Y per business tolerance) should trigger an immediate halt and post-mortem. Escalate failures to the privacy lead and legal counsel for remediation and possible outreach to affected recipients.

## Implementation notes: model prompts, data minimization, and logging

Include precise prompt constraints: ban prompts that ask models to predict sensitive traits, require 'source:' tags for any factual content, and include a fixed template for uncertainty language. Store only the minimum context required for generation and avoid logging raw recipient contents alongside model outputs unless necessary for review.

Log generation metadata: model version, prompt text (redacted for PII), output, reviewer decision, and evidence links. Retention should align with your privacy policy; if uncertain, consult your privacy team. Vendor documentation on enterprise privacy practices may inform defaults but do not substitute for your policy review [5][6].

## Examples and edge cases

Example — Allowed: A variant that uses company size from a verified dataset to craft a subject line. The model's claim about company size must link to that dataset in the experiment record.

Example — Blocked: An A/B variant that instructs a model to infer a recipient's religion from name and locale; this is disallowed because it targets a protected characteristic. Edge case: When a recipient voluntarily provided sensitive info (e.g., health condition) during a form submission, require explicit consent documentation and legal review before using it for personalization.

## Practical checklist

- [ ] Register experiment with hypothesis, owner, data sources, and target sample size.
- [ ] Classify all features as allowed, restricted, or blocked; block inferences about protected traits.
- [ ] Require one verifiable evidence source for any factual claim about a recipient.
- [ ] Run a synthetic dry-run and document a human review pass/fail for each variant.
- [ ] Log model metadata, redacted prompts, outputs, reviewer decision, and evidence links.
- [ ] Set explicit stop conditions (e.g., complaints threshold) and escalation contacts.
- [ ] Minimize logged PII and set retention per privacy policy.
- [ ] Use templated uncertainty language for inferred content; avoid absolute assertions.
- [ ] Obtain privacy/legal sign-off for any use of sensitive or novel data types.
